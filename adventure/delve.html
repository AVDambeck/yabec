Adventure!!!

The main phase of the game is dungeon exploration.

Hazards

The most important resource is time. Each turn, the dungeon master rolls a d6 to determine which hazard occers.

encompases a variety of time based treats to adventurers.
d6 Effect Description
1 Nothing The adventure continues.
2 Burn Torches burn out
3 Fatigue Become fatigued. If already fatigued, take 1d4 damage.
4 Shift Traps reset, doors swing close, incapacitaed creatures wake up, near-death players die, etc.
5-6Encounter Chance of random encounter with a monster.

Searching

Treasure, traps, and secret doors will rarely be obvious at first glance. Searching:

  • Takes a turn.
  • Takes a minimum of three people per zone.
  • Reveals non-obvious features, such as traps.
  • Will always reveal everything it can.

Rest

Taking a turn to rest removes fatigue. If the fatigue hazard occers while you are still fatigued, you will take damage.

Ignore the fatigue hazard while resting.

A wandering monster will disrupt a rest.

A "rest", not to be confused with "camping" and "downtime", does not provide healing. It removes fatigue. See below for methods of healing.

Movement

Moving through an unexplored dungeon at a safe pace: move up to three zones (i.e. three rooms/hallways, or about 120'))

Moving quickly through a familiar area: move up to nine zones instead.

Moving across favorable ground outdoors: 3 miles per hour, while unencumberd, and having camped the night before. After eight hours of travel, each additional hour deals 2 damage componding.

Marching Order

While moving through narrow doors and halls, movement is restricted. The marching order is decided by the players. In general, only the front and the back are significant.

Encounters

Surprise

Players can surprise monsters when opening a door quietly, or charging into a room. Players are unable to surprise monsters when carrying a light in a dark area, and after making a loud noise. Monsters can surprise players when hidden in a room, or after stalking the players for at least one turn.

Surprised creatures take no actions the first round of combat, make a morale check, and attacks against them have advantage (the dungeon master may rule to have automatic hits).

Reaction

The dungeon master might roll to determine monster reaction, especially on random encounters. The dungeon master might include a reaction modifier from either the players in the front or a player initiating a confrontation.

2d6 Result
<=2 Attacks
3–5 Hostile, may attack
6–8 Uncertain, confused
9–11 Indifferent, may negotiate
12 or more Eager, friendly

Evade

When two groups encounter one another, one or both may decide to evade the other, or one group may decide to pursue the evading group.

  • If both choose to evade, nothing happens.
  • If one chooses to evade, and is not pursued, nothing happens.
  • If one chooses to evade, and is pursued, the chance of successfully evading is 3-in-6. If they fail, begin combat.
  • If a group is surprised, they are unable to evade nor pursue.

Wandering Monster

The traditional method calls for a 1-in-6 chance every other turn while in a dungeon. To match theses odds with the hazard die, roll a d4 for each check, with a result of a 1 indicating a monster. I like to use a d6 with the following results: a 1 is always a monster. 2 is an omen, or a monster if it is a dangerous area, if the players have been especially noisy, etc. 3 is always an omen. 4 through 6 are safe.

Monsters appear 1d4 zones away.

Mapping

After the encounter (if any) is settled, players are able to observe the room for long enough to add to their map.

Dungeons are complex environments where it is easy to get lost. Keeping a map is essential. To keep the dungeon mysterious, it is discouraged for the DM to provide an exact map or to correct the players map.

Its recommended that one player take on the role of Mapper. See Roles.

Whiteboard Method

The dungeon master has a clipboard, a page protector, 1/2 or 1/4 graph paper, and a dry erase marker. They can draw out the current and near rooms. It is then up to the players to copy and connect the rooms. As the players adventure deeper into the dungeon, the dungeon master erases and draws new areas.

Flowchart Method

Rather than track the exact dimensions of every room, the mapper might opt to keep a general shape of the dungeon with a loose flow chart, labeling rooms and their connections.

Call Method

Instead of having the DM physically draw rooms, the mapper can instead work out a system of dungeon calls. by which to describe the shape of a dungeon.

While not in combat, the mapper can ask the dungeon master for a call. Each call should have an unambiguous meaning. The mapper and Dungeon Master should work together to establish calls that work for them.

GFC's video is a perfect explanation of dungeon mapping. This page is a written guide based on their advice.

Additionally, you may wish to modify the system to better suit your needs. Any non-ambiguous agreed upon system will work.

The Dungeon Master will only provide calls when the players ask, and only after they enter a room and are able to spend time observing it.

Healing

Healing removes damage.

Bandage

Bandages can be used to provide limited healing. Once an attempt to bandage you has been made, another cannot be made until you take damage. Bandaging also takes one turn. You may bandage yourself, and bandages cannot be reused.

Make an intelligence check. If you succeed, heal the target 1d6, plus a bonus from your WIS.

Magical Healing

Magic potions or spells can provide instantaneous healing.

When magically healing a near death character, they dismember with advantage.

Camping

Sleeping for 8 hours near a campfire, eating a ration, and spending a few hours in study/prayer/recreation: heal 1, or 2 if the ration was cooked into soup with a pot. Spell casters can memorize 1 level of spells (limited by their magic progression table).

Downtime

Enjoying a period of downtime: heal all of your damage, restore ammo/kits, memorize the maximum number of spells allowed by your level, and, if your dungeon master allows downtime actions, take a downtime action for each week.

The length of required downtime is equal to the number of weeks since your last downtime. I.e. if you have been adventuring for five weeks, your downtime must also be five weeks.

You must spend downtime in a town or city, in propper lodging, and pay your upkeep cost for each week.

Evaluate Loyalty

During downtime, hirelings, if any, adjust their loyalty score. For each hireling, make three loyalty checks. For each failure, loose one loyalty. For each success, gain one loyalty (max 10). If they have 10 loyalty, you can reduce it to 5, and gainone renown (max 7).

Light

There are three levels of light:

  • Dark: The darkness is pitch black, and prevents sight. Imposes blindness.
  • Dim: Allows some detail to be seen.
  • Bright: Allows full vision, including details and reading. Disables infravision.

A light source casts bright light within touch, and dim within close.

Number of sources

To illuminate a whole party, one torch is needed for every five adventurers (i.e. PCs, hirelings, and other companions). If there are not enough torches, some members will be in darkness.

Infravision

A special type of vision had by many monsters, and some players: seeing heat energy that radiates of living things. Negate the effect of dark. When a creature with Infravision suddenly goes from dark to bright, they are blinded for one round, and their infravision is disabled until they spend a full turn in the dark.

It is not possible to read in the dim or dark with infravision.

Zones

The dungeon environment is split into zones. As a rule of thumb, each room and hallway is a zone. Large rooms or hallways may be multipole zones.

Guidelines

  • Architecture: Architectural features, such as doorways, should divide zones.
  • 40': Rooms bigger than 40', and hallways longer than 40', should be multiple zones.
  • Even: Large rooms and hallways should have their zones divided evenly.
  • Whole Number: Large rooms and hallways should always have a whole number of zones.

Ranges

Ranges and relative location are divided into four ranges:

  • Touch: Striking distance.
  • Close: Throwing distance. Speaking distance. With in the same or adjacent zone.
  • Distant: Shouting distance. Upto 2 zones away. Farthest distance to make out details (in bright light).
  • Sight: Anything that can be seen. Up to 3 miles in ideal conditions.

Doors

Eavesdropping at a door or corridor can reveal what lies beyond. Roll a d6, and add the bonus from your wisdom score. Some classes, like thieves, have additional bonuses. The dungeon master might roll this behind the screen instead. Make sure to tell them the bonus of the character listening.

On a result of six or more, the dungeon master will reveal sounds you are able to hear.

When opening a door, check your strength. If you fail, the door makes a loud sound. Otherwise, the door opens silently. Only a player at the front of the marching order can open doors; opening a door implies moving to the front. If there is not a door, a player can instead check their dexterity to approach silently.

Physically destroying a wooden door takes a full turn, requires appropriate tools, and makes loud noise.
