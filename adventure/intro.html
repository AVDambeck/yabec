Introduction

This section describes the very basics of how to play Yabec. The following sections will describe detailed game procedures for a variety of situations.

Overview

In Yabec, you play as an adventurer in a fantasy world. You may play a premade character, or make one yourself. This is your avatar, and the way you interact with the game world.

Your character has teamed up with other players' characters (PCs) and adventure. Your primary goal is to gain wealth and power. Great treasure is hidden within deep places of the world, tho many have died in it's search.

The Dungeon Master (DM) is a player with a special role. They are a referee, narrator, and show runner, and play the game world, including all non-player characters (NPCs).

The Game

As a TTRPG, Yabec is a sequence of situations. The DM will describe a situation in the game world as it is observable to your character, about which you may ask clarifying questions.

Eventually the dungeon master will ask you to act based on the information received, sometimes with a time limit. When you make your choice, the dungeon master will resolve it using dice or another game procedure, then advance to another situation.

Yabec is intended as a narrow-roll game. i.e. the dungeon master is more likely to tell you that an action automatically succeeds, or is impossible, comapred to other ttrpgs, that would have had rolls for a wider range of actions.

Scope

The general content of the game is of a fantasy dungeon crawler. Game sessions may often begin and end at the mouth of a dungeon. However, the scope of the game includes an overworld with many, sometimes hidden, dungeons, as well as towns and cities.

Teamwork

In most cases, the party acts as a unit, though many actions, such as attacking, are preformed individually.

The dungeon master should not allow one character to act against the interests of the group.

One player should take on the role of caller. They coridnate the party, get consensus, collate combat actions, and communicate with the dungeon master.

Time

It's critical that the dungeon master, and ideally the players as well, keep close track of time.

The most common unit of time is Turns, sometimes called "dungeon turns". They are about 10 mintues of in-game time. Each turn, many things happen in the background: torches burn slowly, monsters lurk in the darkness, guards go on patrol. The dungeon master will knock twice on the table to subtly convey the end of a turn, and will announce hazards that come with it if there are any.

Rounds and Initiative

Within a turn, when there is a great time pressure, such as during a chase or during combat, the dungeon master will announce the start of (initiative).

During initiative, time is primarily measured in rounds, which last around 60 seconds.

At the start of each round, the dungeon master and the players each roll a d6. If the players roll higher or equal to the DM, they go first. Otherwise, the monsters go first.

Players order their actions however they wish, acting simultaneously, in sequence, or in some other way. Each round, each character can take only one action. Then they must wait for the next round to act again.

Optional: Individual Initiative / Willed Actions

Instead of annoucing initiative, the dungeon master will announce willing of actions at the start of each round. Players discuss, and then announce the type of action they wish to take. The initiative die they roll is on the table below.

Players who roll a four or higher act simultaniously before monsters. Players who roll three or lower act simultaniously after monsters.

Checks

When you dungeon master asks you for a check, roll the appropriate dice (often a d20) and try to roll less than or equal to the target number. Tell the dungeon master if you succeed or fail.

The most common type of check is an ability check, which uses a d20 against your score in that ability. which uses a d20 against your score in that ability. For example, when strength check is called for, roll a twenty sided die. If your character has an 8 in strength, and you roll an 8 or less, you succeed the check.

Attacking

Attack checks (or attack rolls) have a have a hidden target number called an armor class, or AC. If the dungeon master has not announced the AC of a monster, simply tell the DM the value of your check.

To check your attack, take your AV (attack value) and subtract a d20. If it is less than or equal to the enemy's AC, you succeed in your hit and must roll damage.

Dodging

Dodge checks (or dodge rolls) have a hidden target number called an attack value, or AV. If the DM has not announced the AV of a monster, simply tell the dungeon master the value of your check.

To check your dodge, roll a d20 and add your AC. If it is less than or equal to the AV, you succeed and dodge the attack.

Advantage and Disadvantage

When making a check with advantage, attempt twice. If you succeed on either roll, you succeed the check.

When making a roll with disadvantage, attempt twice. If you fail on either roll, you fail the check.

You cannot have multiple stacks of advantage nor disadvantage. Advantageand and disadvantage cancel each other out.
