Introduction

This section describes the very basics of how to play Yabec, as well as its design philosophy. The following sections will describe game procedures for a variety of situations.

Roleplay

In Yabec, you play as an adventurer in a fantasy world. You may play a premade character, or make one yourself. This is your avatar, and the way you interact with the game world.

Party

Your character will team up with other players' characters (PCs) and adventure. Your primary goal is to gain wealth and power.

The Dungeon Master

The Dungeon Master (DM) is a player with a special role. They are a referee, narrator, and show runner, and play the game world, including all non-player characters (NPCs).

The Game

As a TTRPG, Yabec is a sequence of situations. The DM will describe a situation in the game world as it is observable to your character, about which you may ask clarifying questions.

Eventually the dungeon master will ask you to act based on the information received, sometimes with a time limit. When you make your choice, the dungeon master will resolve it using dice or another game procedure, then advance to another situation.

Teamwork

In most cases, the group acts as a unit, however sometimes characters do act individually.

The group should act in a coordinated manner. The dungeon master should not allow one character to act against the interests of the group, and will often wait to resolve one character's actions until the actions of the group are settled.

Adventure

Most adventures begin in a town. There, players can talk to the inhabitants, buy equipment, and search for rumors.

Once everyone is ready, players group up to delve into a dungeon.

In the dungeon they map its hallways, search its rooms, and try to steal its treasure without being killed.

Once the party is ready to leave, they return to town to split the spoils.

The world is large, so players may even travel to a different area of the world.

The Three Games

This basic game is exclusively a dungeon crawl. Players go room by room, clearing floor after floor.

The advanced game expands the perview of the advenure. Players also explore towns and complete multiple quest lines, perhaps a negotation with a local ruler, or a journey to multiple adventuring locations.

The expert game makes players powerful rulers, and parts of the game world itself. Players build strongholds, raise armies, and negotate with kings.

Turns

The most important resource is time. Turns, sometimes called "dungeon turns", are about 10 mintues of in-game time. Each turn, many things happen in the background: torches burn slowly, monsters lurk in the darkness, guards go on patrol. The dungeon master will knock twice on the table to subtly convey the end of a turn, and will announce events that come with it if there are any.

Rounds and Initiative

Within a turn, when there is a great time pressure, such as during a chase or during combat, the dungeon master will announce the start of combat (initiative).

During initiative, time is primarily measured in rounds, which last around 5 seconds.

At the start of each round, the dungeon master and the players each roll a d6. If the players roll higher or equal to the DM, they go first. Otherwise, the monsters go first.

Each round, each character can take only one action. Then they must wait for the next round to act again.

Optional: Individual Initiative / Willed Actions

Instead of annoucing initiative, the dungeon master will announce willing of actions at the start of each round. Players discuss, and then announce the type of action they wish to take. The initiative die they roll is on the table below.

Players who roll a four or higher act before monsters. Players who roll three or lower act after.

Checks

When you dungeon master asks you for a check, roll the appropriate dice (d20 unless said otherwise) and try to roll less than or equal to the target number. Tell the dungeon master if you succeed or fail.

The most common type of check is an ability check, but you may also have to roll under your physical or magical saves.

Attacking

Attack checks (or attack rolls) have a have a hidden target number called an armor class, or AC. If the dungeon master has not announced the AC of a monster, simply tell the DM the value of your check.

To check your attack, take your AV (attack value) and subtract a d20. If it is less than or equal to the enemy's AC, you succeed in your hit and must roll damage.

Dodging

Dodge checks (or dodge rolls) have a hidden target number called an attack value, or AV. If the DM has not announced the AV of a monster, simply tell the dungeon master the value of your check.

To check your dodge, roll a d20 and add your AC. If it is less than or equal to the AV, you succeed and dodge the attack.

Advantage and Disadvantage

When making a check with advantage, attempt twice. If you succeed on either roll, you succeed the check.

When making a roll with disadvantage, attempt twice. If you fail on either roll, you fail the check.

You cannot have multiple stacks of advantage or disadvantage, and they cancel each other out.
