War Games

In the days of yore, aristocracy developed games to simulate battlefield stratagy. Games such as chess developed from this tradition. By the 1950's, war gaming had developed into a simulationist hobby, attempting to recreate great battles, such as those of the nepoleonic wars, using painted minatures and dice.

In 1969, in the Twin Cities, David Westly invited several people over for a noval war game. It was a multi-objective game set in the fictional German town of Braustein. Unlike other games at the time, where players abstractly directed dozens of units, this new game had individual and non-military roles, such as mayor and univerity chancellor. However, nearly 20 players showecd up for the game, forcing Westly to improvise.

Although Westly initially considered the game a failure, it was imencly well recieved, and four additional sessions were run him, and Dave Arneson. When Westly departed for the army in 1970, Arneson begane a fantasy game set in the mideval Blackmoor, where players quested for magic and gold, and vanquished the forces of evil. As part of this game, he borrowed from Chainmail...

Influenced by several other midevil war games, Gary Gygax and Jeff Perren developed a miniature wargame called Chainmail, which first saw print through Guidon Games in 1971. It also critically included a 14 page fantasy supplement, drawing from the works of Tolkien, and Moorcock.

Gygax and Don Kaye formed Tactical Studies Rules in 1973, to publish the first edition of Dungeons and Dragons which saw print in 1974. This first "brown book" edition was an attempt to publish a fantasy-themed Braustein-style game. TSR, then operated out of Gygax's bacement, suddenly found huge market success, and went to publish many, many games.

Dungeons and Dragons™

Some of the challenges faced by TSR were: the limitations of publishing technology at the time, the limited ability of game writing methods, the lack of knowlage the public had of the game, and many others.

The brown book, and its expansions (sometimes called "0e"), where natoriously hard to understand (leading to many other adventure games, including Tunnels and Trolls). TSR tasked Dr Holmes with wroting an introductory game, published in 1977. This is known as the "blue book", Holmes Basic, or Basic Set 1st version. It only covered 1st to 3rd level and had simplified rules. TSR editors also may have included portions of the yet unreleased AD&D into it.

AD&D was made for a bunch of reasons. The player's handbook, with it's iconic statue gem eye stealing artwork, was published in 1978. It's significant for being the first edition published soley under Gygax's name. He and Arneson had fallen into conflict, and there were several lawsuits. (Arneson went on to have various other contributions to RPG culture, and computer education). It also include's Gygax's new philisophy on what D&D should be. Earlier D&D products where ambigous and difficult to parse. This lead to most people improvising (just as Westly had). However, as D&D was become a product line of various expansions, adventures, and liscensed products, as well as a competitive turniment games, Gygax sought to write a rules system which could unambiously cover every possible situation.

The boundries of AD&D 1e are ambigious and debated. Some people site Unearthed Arcana and Oriental Adventures as being a 1.5e, as they were so different. Reguardless, AD&D 1e was published from 1977 until 1989, and is the begining of the "Classic" culture of play.

Fun Fact: Some versions were unable to include dice due to supply shortages. They instead included "chits". They were small pieces of wood, plastic, or even paper. The chits would be placed in an olaque bag, and one could be randomly selected to produce random numbers.

However, the Holmes Basic set had already introduced many players to the game, who either refused to switch to AD&D, or adopted it's content ad hoc (further diversifying the scene). To support the Basic D&D system, a new product was introduced in 1977, to extend the game upto 14th level. It was edited by Tom Moldvay, Frank Mentzer, and Troy Denning, It had two books: "basic" which was levels 1-3, and "expert" using levels 4 through 14. This system became known as Moldvay Basic, or Basic Set 2nd version. It featured several iconic dragon images (the 1981 version being the most iconic). It's somewhat different than Holmes Basic, and very different from AD&D. Its original printing included dungeon "geomorphs", dice, and lists of monsters and treasure. Later versions included In Search of the Unknown, or The Keep on The Borderland.

To further expand the BD&D supersystem, TSR later publuehd the Basic Set 3rd version. These books took on a different approach to teaching the gamed (remember the limitations in game writing). The books read as choose-your-own-adventure books that teach you a version of basic Dungeons and Dragons. This system was published over five books: Basic, Expert, Comapnion, Master, and Immortal. IT went upto level 36, and is known as BECMI Basic.

Titter Pigs

This is a brief history, and largely dominated by the brand-name product.

Tunnels and Trolls published by Flying Buffalo in 1975 was the first TTRPG not published by TSR (although Empire of the Petal Throne was released at the same time and began independant). Dragon Warriors published by Corgi Books in 1985 was the first TTRPG to use slots for inventory.

Metamophis Alpha (1976) was the first sci-fi TTRPG, and Superhero: 2044 (1977) was the first superhero TTRPG. LARP also developed sometime in the 1970s.

Call of Cthulhu (1981) was based on Basic Role-Playing (1980), and is the first game ith the written intent to create the experience of a horror story. This is one of the first games targeting the "Traditional" culture of play.

Computer RPGs, such as Rogue (1980) were also published, often as directed adaptations of their programmers' D&D games.

The Satanic Panic happened in the mid 80s, and had a huge impact on gaming culture. For example, many TSR books have "Ten'ari, instead of demons.

Edition Wars

In 1989, TSR was publishing and liscensing Advanced Dungeons and Dragons, which now spread across a dozen books, each with small changes, and new systems. They also published Basic Dungeons and Dragons, which also included "expert", but not "advanced". To remedy this, second edition, or 2e, was released. The best reference for this system is the 1991 Rules Cyclopedia.

By 1997, TSR was nearly bankrupt, and was purchase by Wizards of the Coast, which was and is owned by multi-national corperatoin Hasbro. They published 3e in 2000. This saw the intoduction of the d20 now iconic d20 system, as well as the Open Game Liscense (alghough the Dominion Rules published in 1999 was the first open source rules. They also had the amazing idea to sell more books by having players, not just dungeon masters, buy them. This resulted in an unmanageable 200 books in the system, and characters being ungodly combos of desperate options.

Trouble started in 2008 with the release of the controversial 4th edition, which was generally recieved negatively.

Some people liked 3e. They largely stayed with it, or switched to games like Pathfinder, which further developed the tactical culture of play.

Some people instead played narritive games, such as Apacolypse World. These games place story first, rather than game play, and focus one rules that align with their desired fiction.

However, some people began to try and play games like that volitile, diverse period of the 1980s...

Old-School Rennaisannce

The various TSR games were still around, and distributed widely on the intenret. However, they are dated, difficult to read, and have various innate problems. Retro games, such as HackMaster (2001) and Castles & Crusades (2004) were became popular. Then came games targeting a romantizied view of the 80s, such as Labyrinth Lord (2007).

Zines and blogs began discussing the OSR, and making contributions. Drive Through RPG supported indi publishers with digital and on-demand printing.

Other nugen games include the Black Hack, Knave, Morg Borg,a and Carn.
