War Games

In the days of yore, aristocracy developed games to simulate battlefield stratagy, such as chess. By the 1950's, war gaming had developed, attempting to acurately recreate great battles, such as those of the nepoleonic wars, using painted minatures and dice.

In 1969, in the Twin Cities, David Westly invited several people over for a noval, multi-objective game set in the fictional German town of Braustein. Unlike other games at the time, it had individual and non-military roles, such as mayor and univerity chancellor. Nearly 20 players showed up for the game, forcing Westly to improvise.

Although Westly initially considered the game a failure, four additional sessions were run him and Dave Arneson. When Westly departed for the army in 1970, Arneson began a fantasy game set in the mideval Blackmoor. Players quested for magic and gold, and vanquished the forces of evil. As part of this game, he borrowed from Chainmail...

Influenced by several other war games, Gary Gygax and Jeff Perren developed a miniature wargame called Chainmail, which first saw print through Guidon Games in 1971. It included a 14 page fantasy supplement, drawing from the works of Tolkien, and Moorcock.

Gygax worked witn Arneson to publish a version of his Blackmoor game. In 1974, Tactical Studies Rules published the first edition of Dungeons and Dragons. This first "brown book" edition is the first published TTRPG. TSR, then operated out of Gygax's bacement, suddenly found huge market success, and went to publish many, many games.

Dungeons and Dragons™

Some of the challenges faced by TSR were: the limitations of publishing technology at the time, the limited ability of game writing methods, the lack of knowlage the public had of the unique game, and many others.

The brown book, and its expansions (sometimes called "0e"), where natoriously hard to understand (leading to many other adventure games, including Tunnels and Trolls). TSR tasked Dr Holmes with writing an introductory game, published in 1977. This is known as the "blue book", Holmes Basic, or Basic Set 1st version. It only covered 1st to 3rd level and had simplified rules. TSR editors also may have included portions of the yet unreleased AD&D into it.

AD&D was made for a bunch of reasons. The player's handbook, with it's iconic statue gem eye stealing artwork, was published in 1978. Is significant for being the first edition published soley under Gygax's name as he and Arneson had a falling out. (Arneson went on to have various other contributions to RPG culture, and computer education). It also include's Gygax's new philisophy on what D&D should be. Most people improvised most of the game, (just as Westly had). However, as D&D was become a product line of various expansions, adventures, liscensed products, and even a competitive circut. Gygax sought to write a rules system which could unambiously cover every possible situation.

The boundries of AD&D 1e are ambigious and debated. Some people site Unearthed Arcana and Oriental Adventures as being a 1.5e, as they were so different. Reguardless, AD&D 1e was published from 1977 until 1989, and is the begining of the "Classic" culture of play.

Fun Fact: Some versions were unable to include dice due to supply shortages. They instead included "chits". They were small pieces of wood, plastic, or even paper. The chits would be placed in an olaque bag, and one could be randomly selected to produce random numbers.

However, the Holmes Basic set had already introduced many players to the game, who either refused to switch to AD&D, or adopted it's content ad hoc. To support the Basic D&D system, a 1977 product extended the game upto 14th level. It was edited by Tom Moldvay, Frank Mentzer, and Troy Denning. It had two books: "basic" which was levels 1-3, and "expert" using levels 4 through 14. This system became known as Moldvay Basic, or Basic Set 2nd version. It featured several iconic dragon images (the 1981 version being the most iconic). It's somewhat different than Holmes Basic, and very different from AD&D. Later versions came bundled with In Search of the Unknown, or The Keep on The Borderland.

To further complicate the BD&D supersystem, TSR later published the Basic Set 3rd version. These books took on a different approach to teaching the game as choose-your-own-adventure book. This system was published over five books: Basic, Expert, Comapnion, Master, and Immortal. It went upto level 36, and is known as BECMI Basic.

Dungeons and Dragons has become the Klenex of Table Top Role Playing Games. It's perspectives, biases, and problems dominate the space, even to this day. Some other titter pigs of note are:

Tunnels and Trolls published by Flying Buffalo in 1975 was the first TTRPG not published by TSR.

Dragon Warriors published by Corgi Books in 1985 was the first TTRPG to use slots for inventory.

Metamophis Alpha (1976) was the first sci-fi TTRPG, and Superhero: 2044 (1977) was the first superhero TTRPG. LARP also developed sometime in the 1970s.

Call of Cthulhu (1981) was based on Basic Role-Playing (1980), and is the first game ith the written intent to create the experience of a horror story. This is one of the first games targeting the "Traditional" culture of play.

Computer RPGs, such as Rogue (1980) were also published, often as directed adaptations of their programmers' D&D games.

The Satanic Panic happened in the mid 80s, and had a huge impact on gaming culture. For example, many TSR books have "Ten'ari, instead of demons.

the Dominion Rules published in 1999 was the first open source rules.

Edition Wars

In 1989, TSR was publishing and liscensing Advanced Dungeons and Dragons, which spread across a dozen books. They also published Basic Dungeons and Dragons, which confusingly included "expert", but not "advanced". To remedy this, second edition, or 2e, was released. The best reference for this system is the 1991 Ru.les Cyclopedia.

By 1997, TSR was nearly bankrupt, and was purchase by Wizards of the Coast, which was and is owned by multi-national scum-lord Hasbro. They published 3e in 2000. This saw the intoduction of the iconic d20 system, and the Open Game Liscense. They also had the amazing idea to sell more books by publishing hundreds of character options. This resulted in an unmanageable 200 books in the system, and characters being ungodly combos of desperate options, which took hours to assemble.

Trouble started in 2008 with the release of 4th edition, which was recieved negatively.

Some people liked 3e. They largely stayed with it, or switched to games like Pathfinder, which further developed the tactical culture of play.

Some people instead played narritive games, such as Apacolypse World. These games place story first, rather than game play, and focus one rules that align with their desired fiction.

However, some people began to try and play games like that volitile, diverse period of the 1980s...

Old-School Rennaisannce

The various TSR games were still around, and distributed widely on the intenret. However, they are dated, difficult to read, and have various innate problems. Retro games, such as HackMaster (2001) and Castles & Crusades (2004) were became popular. Then came games targeting a romantizied view of the 80s, such as Labyrinth Lord (2007).

Zines and blogs began discussing the OSR, and making contributions. Drive Through RPG supported indi publishers with digital and on-demand printing.

Other nugen games include the Black Hack, Knave, Morg Borg, GLOG, and Carn.

YABEC owes much of it's heritage to Old-School Essentials, and by extension Moldvey Basic. OSE, writtn by Gavin Norman and published by Necrotic Gnome, is a TTRPG ecosystem aiming to be faithful to the 1e games, while using modern game writing.

YABEC also inherits significantly from the blog culture and it's many GLOG hacks. The goblin laws of gaming aim to be rules light, hackable, and brutal. They're mostly distributed via blogs, and Google Docs.
